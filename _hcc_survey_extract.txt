a b s t r a c t
Wireless networks have become integral to modern communication systems, enabling the seamless
exchange of information across a myriad of applications. However, the inherent characteristics of
wireless channels, such as fading, interference, and openness, pose significant challenges to achieving
fault-tolerant consensus within these networks. Fault-tolerant consensus, a critical aspect of distributed
systems, ensures that network nodes collectively agree on a consistent value even in the presence of
faulty or compromised components. This survey paper provides a comprehensive overview of fault-
tolerant consensus mechanisms specifically tailored for wireless networks. We explore the diverse
range of consensus protocols and techniques that have been developed to address the unique chal-
lenges of wireless environments. The paper systematically categorizes these consensus mechanisms
based on their underlying principles, communication models, and fault models. It investigates how
these mechanisms handle various types of faults, including communication errors, node failures,
and malicious attacks. It highlights key use cases, such as sensor networks, Internet of Things (IoT)
applications, wireless blockchain, and vehicular networks, where fault-tolerant consensus plays a
pivotal role in ensuring reliable and accurate data dissemination.

1. Introduction
The fault-tolerant consensus pertains to the ability of a dis-
tributed computing system to reach an agreement or make a
decision even the presence of faulty components or communi-
cation failures. In a distributed system, where multiple nodes or
components work together to achieve a common goal, failures
can occur due to hardware malfunctions, network issues, or other
reasons. Achieving consensus means ensuring that all nodes in
the system agree on a particular value or decision, even in the
face of these failures. This is crucial for maintaining the reliability
and correctness of distributed systems. As wireless networks have
become more prevalent and diverse, there is a growing need to
ensure fault-tolerant consensus in these networks as well. Wire-
less networks, such as the IoT networks, ad hoc networks, and
vehicular networks (used in smart cars), have unique challenges
compared to traditional wired networks. These challenges include
limited bandwidth, variable signal strengths, and the potential

1.1. Importance and significance of WFTC
As the modern network complexity keeps increasing, wireless
networks often need to accommodate a large number of devices
and users spread across a wide geographical area. A distributed
framework allows for easier scaling as new devices can be added
without significant changes to the overall network architecture.
In addition, wireless networks are susceptible to various types
of interference, signal loss, and node failures. By distributing
the network functionality, the impact of failures or disturbances
can be localized, and the network can continue functioning in
the presence of such issues. As a result, distributed computing
has emerged as an efficient and predominant trend in wireless
networks.
In a distributed framework, devices need to achieve agreement
through periodic information exchange for task allocation and
decision-making. This process is also referred to as consensus [1].

1.2. Development of WFTC
The first significant work that introduced the concept of con-
sensus in distributed systems is often attributed to Leslie Lam-
port’s work [6]. This paper introduced the concept of the Paxos
algorithm, which is a fundamental algorithm for achieving con-
sensus in distributed systems. Paxos addresses the consensus
problem by allowing a set of distributed nodes to make an agree-
ment on a single value, even in the presence of faults or failures.
The algorithm defines a protocol for reaching agreement and
handles scenarios where nodes may fail, messages would be
postponed, and other complications may arise. The name ‘‘Paxos’’
was inspired by the Greek island of Paxos, which is known for its
history of democracy and agreement. Leslie Lamport’s Paxos algo-
rithm has had an important influence on the field of distributed
systems and has inspired numerous variations and adaptations.
It remains a foundational concept for achieving consensus in dis-
tributed computing environments. Since then, other algorithms
like Raft and Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT) have been
developed to address the consensus problem as well. Raft [7]
consensus protocol is a classical non-byzantine fault-tolerant al-
gorithm, which is usually adopted in distributed databases and
blockchains. Developed by Castro et al. [8], PBFT stands out as the
pioneering Byzantine Fault Tolerance (BFT) consensus protocol
reliant on state machine replication, renowned for its practical vi-
ability. It has practically become synonymous with BFT consensus
within the blockchain sphere due to its widespread recognition
and adoption [9–11].
Thereafter, existing works consider the consensus problem in
the context of wireless networks. In [12], Zou et al. propose a
novel Proof-of-Communication (PoC) consensus protocol, which
is able to achieve k-times consensus among n devices in wireless
networks within O(k + logn) time steps with high probabil-
ity. Besides, Jing et al. [13] were the pioneers in addressing
the challenge of majority consensus in the context of Byzantine
fault-prone edge devices operating over an unreliable wireless
channel. Their work encompassed tackling malicious faults orig-
inating from Byzantine devices across the physical, protocol, and
information layers simultaneously.
1.3. Comparison with existing surveys
Explored in the work by Gao et al. [14], is the consensus
control challenge faced by multi-agent systems. This paper de-
livers a comprehensive survey of present findings pertaining to
fault-tolerant consensus control challenges, structured under a
novel classification. This classification dissects physical security-
preserving techniques into categories like actuator faults, sensor
faults, and compound faults [15,16]. Additionally, logical security-
preserving techniques are outlined encompassing aspects such as
data availability, integrity, and confidentiality.
Xiao et al. [17] conduct an extensive survey of distributed
consensus protocols tailored to blockchain networks. This survey
offers a comprehensive assessment and analysis of contemporary
blockchain consensus protocols, taking into account their histor-
ical evolution. The study places particular emphasis on scruti-
nizing aspects such as performance, fault tolerance, and security
ramifications. Their sources encompass a variety of materials,
including academic papers, white papers on consensus proto-
cols, official documentation, and statistics from cryptocurrency
websites.
In [18], Qin et al. research the latest strides achieved in
the field of consensus among multi-agent systems. This survey
takes a control-focused approach, intended to offer interested
researchers an inclusive perspective on recent advancements
in multi-agent system consensus. The authors aim to fill gaps
that were previously unexplored or insufficiently detailed in
earlier review papers, even though they have garnered significant
attention and interest over the recent years.
In [19], Ren et al. provide an overview of consensus challenges
within the context of multi-agent coordination. The central ob-
jective of this survey is to encapsulate the recent advancements
in consensus issues within the cooperative control domain. The
overarching aim is to streamline research efforts in this field and
promote progress.
In summary, these surveys consider the fault-tolerant con-
sensus problem on multi-agent systems or blockchain networks.
These studies exhibit various shortcomings in distinct areas of
comparison, particularly concerning aspects such as the design
of consensus protocols. In addition, to the best of our knowledge,
there is still no survey on he fault-tolerant consensus in WFTC.
Compared with the blockchain and multi-agent scenarios, the
differences of wireless network scenarios on fault-tolerant con-
sensus can be attributed to the communication medium, physical
layer consideration, and mobility. Therefore, we consider it both
opportune and beneficial to offer a structured overview of WFTC
at this juncture.
Organization. The rest of this survey is organized as follows.

2. Fundamental issues of WFTC
In this section, we first present the definitions and properties
in WFTC. Then, we consider the failures in WFTC, including the
non-byzantine failures in WFTC, the byzantine failures in WFTC,
and the summary based on a three-layer failure model.
2.1. The definitions and properties in WFTC
The goal of the conventional consensus problem in a dis-
tributed system is to achieve unanimous agreement among all
nodes within the system regarding certain shared data values.
As analogous to the classical distributed consensus, the wireless
consensus protocols endeavor to make all participating nodes in
the wireless network agree on the common network decision.
However, several published works achieve a consensus among
all participating nodes based on the reliable wireless channel,
such as [20] and [21]. Whereas, in realistic wireless networks, the
shared wireless channel is unreliable and fault-prone. This survey
paper examines a binary consensus challenge within wireless

networks that face faults. To be specific, the situation involves
n agents positioned in a three-dimensional Euclidean space. The
initial arrangement of agents and their subsequent movements
during each round can take on arbitrary configurations, with the
sole restriction being that all agents remain within each other’s
transmission range. For any given event, an agent v has the
liberty to either support or oppose it. This preference is indicated
by the parameter Xv, where Xv = 1 signifies agreement and
Xv = 0 denotes disagreement. The realm of faulty agents harbors
uncertainty and unpredictability, operating under the assumed
maximum of f faulty agents within the wireless network. The
misbehavior of a faulty agent might manifest as either a crash
or a declaration of an inaccurate value.
By engaging in a discourse regarding the event with their
peers, the non-faulty agents within the wireless network ulti-
mately converge upon a unanimous viewpoint, solidifying it as
the ultimate agreement pertaining to that specific event. When
striving for consensus, the following fundamental properties ought
to guarantee for a wireless consensus when faults occur in dis-
tributed systems.
• Termination: Every non-faulty process decides an output.
This means that the consensus algorithm must ensure that
the process of reaching consensus eventually concludes,
even if some nodes are faulty or the communication net-
work is unreliable.
• Agreement: All non-faulty nodes should ultimately agree on
the same value or decision. This means that the consen-
sus algorithm should prevent situations where some nodes
decide on one value while others decide on a different value.
• Validity: The agreed-upon value should be one of the values
initially proposed by a correct node. In other words, the
consensus algorithm should not allow nodes to agree on
values that were not proposed by any correct node.
• Liveness: The consensus algorithm is supposed to make
progress in reaching consensus as long as the majority of
nodes are correct and the network is sufficiently connected.
Liveness ensures that the algorithm does not get stuck in a
state where no progress can be made.
• Fault Tolerance: The consensus algorithm should be resilient
to the presence of a certain number of faulty nodes or
Byzantine faults. Even when a subset of nodes behaves arbi-
trarily, the algorithm should still ensure agreement among
the correct nodes.
2.2. The failures considered in WFTC
2.2.1. The non-byzantine failures in WFTC
The non-byzantine fault-tolerant consensus problem focuses
mainly on the non-byzantine faults on participating nodes. The
non-byzantine faults would be caused by software bugs, hard-
ware malfunctions, or network issues [22], but not by malicious
or arbitrary attacks/behavior. Specifically, the non-byzantine fail-
ures in WFTC can be categorized into the physical layer, protocol
layer, and data layer, as introduced in the work [23]. Jing and Zou
et al. examined the adverse effects caused by Byzantine nodes
across the physical, protocol, and data layers. They posited that
these Byzantine nodes possessed knowledge about the history
and current status of protocol execution in each round and had
the liberty to act arbitrarily within the physical, protocol, and data
layers. Furthermore, they assumed that these Byzantine nodes
acted rationally, implying that if a particular behavior would
directly expose them to detection by at least f + 1 loyal nodes,
they would refrain from engaging in it. Consequently, when cat-
egorizing Byzantine failures in WFTC concerning their negative
behaviors, these failures can be sorted into the physical, protocol,
and data layers.
Physical layer. The non-byzantine failures at the physical
layer predominantly present themselves as inadvertent failures,
such as hardware malfunctions, power outages, or unintentional
misconfigurations. These failures can lead to unexpected disrup-
tions in the system’s operation, ultimately impacting performance
and reliability adversely. For example, under crash failures or
transient failures, Bhusalmith et al. [24] shown that the non-
faulty nodes may be temporarily disrupted and cannot achieve
agreement. Besides, with respect to accidental failures in wireless
sensor networks, Liu et al. [2] told us that the unreliable wireless
communication can cause message loss and node failures.
Protocol layer. Non-byzantine fault-tolerant consensus mech-
anisms are often used at the protocol layer in various distributed
systems. In a layered architecture, the protocol layer handles
the communication and coordination between different nodes or
components within a distributed system. Protocols for achieving
non-byzantine fault-tolerant consensus are crucial in this layer
because they enable nodes to agree on decisions or values despite
the presence of failures, delays, or malicious behavior. These
consensus protocols provide a foundation for reliable communi-
cation and cooperation in distributed systems. For instance, in
blockchain technology, which is widely used in cryptocurrencies
and decentralized applications, consensus protocols like PoW and
PoS are employed to ensure that the network nodes agree on
the order and validity of transactions. Additionally, the work
Raft [25] considered the consensus problem for blockchains using
non-byzantine fault-tolerant algorithms.
Data layer. Different from the physical layer, the non-byzantine
failures at the data layer or information layer emphasize that
the participating nodes have the capability to send messages
containing incorrect, erroneous, or empty opinions. This manip-
ulation is intended to deceive honest nodes in the pursuit of
achieving a majority consensus outcome. For instance, in dis-
tributed database systems, consensus mechanisms can be used
to ensure that updates, inserts, and deletions to the database are
agreed upon by all relevant nodes. This helps prevent issues like
data inconsistencies or conflicts that could arise due to network
issues or node failures, e.g., fake messages. In [26], Koponen et al.
managed non-Byzantine failures in distributed databases. When
non-Byzantine node crashes or network partitioning happened, it
is impossible to ensure the consensus in WFTC.
Apart from the above three-layer failure model, the non-
byzantine failures in WFTC can also occur across multiple layers.
In this survey, the terminology of ‘‘multiple layers’’ means that
NBFTC mechanisms are implemented across more than one layer
of the network stack. For example, the integration of the pro-
tocol layer and application layer poses a series of opportunities
for implementing non-byzantine fault-tolerant consensus prob-
lem. In [27], the abstract MAC layer is viewed as an attractive
tool to simplify the non-byzantine fault-tolerant consensus issue.
This tool implies that the physical failures do not destroy the
application layer.
2.2.2. The byzantine failures in WFTC
The byzantine failures are a crucial aspect of the consensus
problem in distributed systems. The term ‘‘Byzantine failures’’
pertains to a scenario where some nodes in a distributed network
can exhibit arbitrary and malicious behavior, including sending
contradictory or incorrect information to other nodes. This term
originates from the ‘‘Byzantine Generals’ Problem’’, a thought
experiment introduced by Leslie Lamport [5]. Similar to the above
non-byzantine failures, the byzantine failures in WFTC can also be
classified into three layers: the physical layer, protocol layer, and
data layer.
Physical layer. Byzantine attacks at the physical layer in WFTC
refer to a type of security threat in which malicious actors at-
tempt to compromise the physical components of a communica-
tion system in order to disrupt or manipulate the communication

Problem, a theoretical problem in distributed computing that
deals with the consensus issue among the participating nodes
when existing some faulty or malicious nodes. In the context of
the physical layer, Byzantine attacks can involve various forms
of tampering with hardware, signals, or electromagnetic waves
to achieve their goals, i.e., physical tampering, signal jamming,
and Signal Spoofing. For example, Moniz et al. in [28] consider
the integration of Byzantine nodes and dynamic omission trans-

non-byzantine nodes. As yet another example, Santoro et al. [29]
consider the dynamic Byzantine faults in WFTC.
Protocol layer. At the protocol layer, Byzantine attacks can
exploit vulnerabilities in communication protocols to undermine
the integrity, availability, or confidentiality of network communi-
cations. Common Byzantine attacks at the protocol layer include
but are not limited to sybil attacks, Data Injection, and fork-
ing attacks. Byzantine attacks, such as Denial of Service (DoS)
attacks, can overwhelm network resources, rendering the net-
work unavailable to legitimate users. This disrupts the normal
flow of communication and can have financial and operational
implications in WFTC. To counter Byzantine attacks at the pro-
tocol layer, various security measures are employed, including
firewalls, secure protocol design, and intrusion detection and
prevention systems (IDS/IPS). For instance, Buterin et al. [30]
consider a combination of PoS principles and trustless Byzantine
faults on blockchain networks.
Data layer. In the context of fault-tolerant consensus prob-
lems, Byzantine attacks at data layer of information layer involve
malicious actors manipulating or corrupting data or information
in a distributed system to undermine consensus, compromise
security, or deceive other participants. Byzantine attacks at this
layer are able to take various forms, including data tampering
(attackers create multiple fake identities or nodes to gain in-
fluence over the consensus process), false reporting (Malicious
nodes intentionally report incorrect or misleading information to
disrupt the consensus process or achieve personal gain), and data
injection (attackers inject malicious data or false information into
the system to deceive other nodes and influence the consensus
outcome). Take the previous work [31] as an example, Chen
et al. consider the data tampering attack in the data layer. The
data tampering attack has huge damage to the signature and
verification processes in a blockchain network. Overall, Byzantine
attacks at the data or information layer pose a serious threat
to the integrity and reliability of distributed systems. Preventive
measures and well-designed consensus mechanisms are essen-
tial to mitigate these attacks and ensure the accuracy of shared
information.
On top of the above three layers, the byzantine-failures are
able to happen across multiple layers in WFTC. Byzantine attacks
across multiple layers can have a cascading effect, where an at-
tack at one layer can amplify the impact of attacks at other layers.
For example, physical layer attacks can disrupt communication,
making it easier for protocol layer attacks to succeed. Similarly,
information layer attacks can undermine the accuracy of data
used by protocol layer mechanisms. Mitigating Byzantine at-
tacks across multiple layers requires a comprehensive approach,
e.g., layer-specific defenses and cross-layer monitoring. In [32],
Leng et al. address the prevalent concerns (namely, elevated com-
munication overhead, diminished security, limited scalability, and
tracking complexities) related to the practical implementation of
Byzantine fault-tolerant consensus across various layers.
2.2.3. The summary based on three-layer failure model
The work [23] originally defines a three-layer Byzantine failure
model that captures the malicious behaviors of the Byzantine
nodes at the physical, protocol and data layers, such as jamming,
contention, interference, protocol deviation and fake opinions.
The paper also defines the (a, b)-majority consensus problem as
a generalization of the binary majority consensus problem, in
which there are distinct opinions initially and the final agreement
is a b-majority.
Physical layer. At the physical layer, the Byzantine nodes have
the capability to engage in various actions. They can engage in
contention with loyal nodes for access to the wireless channel,
create interference that disrupts the communications of loyal
nodes within the network, or even engage in jamming that di-
rectly affects the physical channel. All these actions contribute
to rendering communications between nodes unreliable, and in
extreme cases, can lead to complete communication failures. It
is important to highlight that each node is furnished with a ba-
sic half-duplex transceiver, allowing them to decipher messages
solely when the received signal strength surpasses a specified
threshold.
Malicious contention, interference, and jamming behaviors
originating from Byzantine nodes are inherently challenging to
detect. An assumption concerning the physical layer is that the
energy possessed by the Byzantine nodes is not limitless. As a
result, we posit that within any interval comprising T rounds
belonging to Ω(n), the Byzantine nodes can potentially jam the
channel for a maximum of ϵ · T rounds, where ϵ < 1/2 is a con-
stant. This limitation is due to energy constraints. This perspective
aligns with prior research, such as [33], which has established
that a condition like ϵ

Protocol layer. At the protocol layer, the Byzantine nodes
possess the capability to deviate arbitrarily from the intended
protocol execution, thereby inducing faults within the network.
Data layer. At the data layer, the Byzantine nodes hold the
ability to dispatch messages that include incorrect, false, or va-
cant opinions. This manipulation is intended to deceive the loyal
nodes regarding the ultimate outcome when striving for majority
consensus. Another critical assumption is that each node, includ-
ing those with Byzantine characteristics, possesses a distinctive
and unmodifiable ID. Consequently, when a listener receives and
decodes a message, they can readily identify the sender, even
if the message holds no meaningful content. This safeguard en-
sures that the Byzantine nodes cannot flood the channel with an
abundance of meaningless messages without raising suspicion,
thereby preventing the successful transmission of messages from
the loyal nodes. The significance of unique IDs in facilitating the
design of effective protocols in Byzantine environments has been
substantiated by prior research [34].
Based on the above presentation, one can conclude that most
of the existing failures in WFTC can be sorted into physical
layer failures, protocol layer failures, and data layer failures. The
fault tolerant consensus problem considered in the works [2,24,
28] focuses mainly on the physical layer failures. Besides, sev-
eral previous works [25,30] study the fault tolerant consensus
issue against layer failures. In addition, some existing works
in [26,31] cope with the failures in WFTC in the context of the
data/information layer.

3. Non-Byzantine fault tolerant consensus
Non-Byzantine Fault Tolerant Consensus (NBFTC) is a class of
consensus algorithms designed to handle non-Byzantine faults,
i.e., faults that do not involve malicious or arbitrary behaviors.
Those non-byzantine faults typically include unintentional faults
resulting from hardware malfunctions, software bugs, or network
issues [22].
Consensus refers to the agreement among a set of devices or
processes (known as nodes) about a particular value or state.
In the context of wireless networks, consensus is crucial for
coordinating actions, ensuring consistent state across nodes, and
tolerating faults [35]. In the case of NBFTC, the consensus is
achieved despite the presence of non-Byzantine faults [22].
The distinction between Byzantine and non-Byzantine faults is
important. Byzantine faults are characterized by arbitrary, poten-
tially malicious behavior, such as a node lying about its state or
sending conflicting information to different nodes. Non-Byzantine
faults, on the other hand, are characterized by nodes failing in
predictable ways, such as crashing (stopping entirely) or omit-
ting (failing to send or receive messages). NBFTC algorithms are
designed to handle these non-Byzantine faults [36].
Fault tolerance is a critical aspect of wireless networks due to
their inherent unreliability and the potential for node or link fail-
ures. NBFTC plays a crucial role in maintaining the performance
and reliability of these networks in the face of such faults [37]. For
example, in a wireless sensor network deployed for environmen-
tal monitoring, nodes might fail due to hardware malfunctions
or energy depletion. An NBFTC algorithm can ensure that the
network continues to function despite these failures, by reach-
ing a consensus on the state of the environment based on the
readings of the functioning nodes [38]. Similarly, in a vehicular ad
hoc network (VANET), nodes (vehicles) might fail to communicate
due to temporary network disruptions or physical obstacles. An
NBFTC algorithm can allow the network to maintain a consistent
view of traffic conditions, thus improving road safety and traffic
efficiency [39].
Implementing NBFTC in wireless networks presents several
unique challenges:
• Node Mobility: In many wireless networks, nodes are mo-
bile. This mobility can lead to rapidly changing network
topologies, making it difficult to maintain a consistent state
across nodes [40].
• Unreliable Communication: Wireless communication is sub-
ject to interference and signal attenuation, leading to un-
predictable message loss. This can make it challenging to
achieve consensus, as nodes may not receive all the neces-
sary messages [22].
• Energy Constraints: Wireless nodes are often battery-powe-
red and need to conserve energy. Consensus algorithms can
be communication-intensive, so designing energy-efficient
NBFTC algorithms is a major challenge [41].
• Scalability: Wireless networks can consist of a large number
of nodes, so NBFTC algorithms need to be scalable. This
means that they should not only perform well as the net-
work size increases but also be robust to a larger number of
potential faults [42].
In spite of these challenges, significant progress has been made
in developing effective NBFTC algorithms for wireless networks,
as we will discuss in the following sections.
3.1. Non-Byzantine WFTC in single layer
In the context of this survey, a ‘‘single layer’’ refers to one
specific layer of the network stack where NBFTC is implemented.
The network stack, also known as the networking model, typically
consists of several layers, including the physical, data link, net-
work, transport, and application layers. Each layer has a specific
function in the process of transmitting data over the network.
In a single-layer NBFTC approach, the fault-tolerance mecha-
nisms are implemented at one specific layer of this stack [22].
In this section, we firstly present several unique challenges for
implementing NBFTC in wireless networks. Then, we introduce
the non-Byzantine WFTC in a single layer. Lastly, we show the
non-Byzantine WFTC across multiple layers.
There are numerous studies addressing various Non-Byzantine
fault models in distributed computing domains, focusing on the
fault-tolerant consensus problem in a single layer. These stud-
ies encompass a wide range of subfields, methodologies, and
solutions.
Starting with the physical layer, Non-Byzantine faults pre-
dominantly manifest as accidental failures, like hardware mal-
functions, power loss, or unintentional misconfigurations. These
failures can cause unexpected interruptions in the system’s func-
tionality, leading to detrimental effects on performance and relia-
bility. Bhusalmith et al. [24] were instrumental in understanding
these issues. Their algorithm was designed to tolerate crash fail-
ures, ensuring that the non-faulty nodes reach agreement even
with unexpected failures. They also extended their research to
look at transient failures, which may be temporary but can still
cause significant disruptions. Similarly, the work of Liu et al. [2]
in wireless sensor networks considered only accidental failures.
They addressed the unreliable nature of wireless communica-
tion, introducing an algorithm that efficiently handles message
loss and node failures due to non-malicious reasons. This par-
ticular study shed light on the often unpredictable nature of
wireless networks, where even minor disturbances can lead to
widespread communication failures. Moreover, in vehicular net-
works, Lee et al. [43] examined the problem of achieving consen-
sus in the presence of transient faults caused by environmental
factors. They proposed a robust consensus algorithm that consid-
ers dynamic network topology and vehicle mobility and delved
into the specific challenges of real-time communication in con-
stantly changing conditions. They also investigated the potential
of employing multiple communication channels to reduce the
likelihood of failure due to environmental interference.
At the protocol layers, extending the discussion to distributed
databases, the work of Koponen et al. [26] is foundational in
managing non-Byzantine failures. They developed protocols to
ensure that a system can reach a consensus and continue oper-
ations even in the face of node crashes or network partitioning.
Their work laid the groundwork for subsequent studies, focusing
on enhancing robustness and scalability in various distributed
computing environments. Zhang et al. [44] investigated fault-
tolerant consensus algorithms that are resilient to non-malicious
failures such as link failures and node crashes. They developed
an adaptive consensus protocol that can dynamically adjust to
varying network conditions, providing an effective solution that
is tailored to the unique challenges of distributed computing,
enhancing the system’s resilience.
At the application layer, Leslie Lamport, in his working pa-
per"The part-time parliament", published in The journal ACM
Transactions on Computer Systems [45]. Paxos is a consistency
algorithm based on message passing, which mainly solves the
problem of how a distributed system agrees on a particular value.
With the development of distributed consensus research, Paxos
algorithm has been widely accepted in academic and industrial
circles. Thompson et al. [46] addressed software bugs and un-
intentional errors within a cloud computing environment. They
utilized a replication technique to handle non-malicious faults,
ensuring uninterrupted functionality even if some replicas en-
countered unexpected errors. This was vital in the context of

to maintaining availability and reliability. They further evalu-

The problem of achieving non-Byzantine fault tolerance in a
single layer spans a broad spectrum of research across various
fields. It includes everything from the design of physical hardware
to application levels, and from working with wireless sensors
to the development of blockchain technologies. The goal is to
create systems that can withstand accidental and non-malicious
breakdowns. The research cited here encompasses a diverse ar-

of this essential research area.
3.2. Non-byzantine WFTC across multiple layers
In the ever-expanding world of network systems and dis-
tributed computing, NBFTC across multiple layers represents an
essential and complex field of study. By extending beyond the
constraints of a single layer, multiple-layer approaches provide a
more comprehensive and versatile understanding of fault-tolerant
consensus, encompassing every aspect of the network stack.
From the intricacies of physical hardware to the sophisticated
algorithms at the application layer, the exploration of NBFTC
across multiple layers yields a rich and multifaceted examination
of technology and computing. In the context of this survey,
‘‘multiple layers’’ refers to an approach where NBFTC mechanisms
are implemented across more than one layer of the network
stack. This can allow for a more comprehensive fault-tolerance
solution, as different types of faults can be handled at the most
appropriate layer [49].
At the base of the physical layer, where the physical layer
interfaces with the protocol layer, NBFTC has been applied to ad-
dress a host of hardware-related problems. Faults at these levels
typically manifest as hardware malfunctions, signal loss, and link
failures [50]. Research in this domain includes the development of
redundant communication paths and mechanisms to detect and
correct errors at the physical layer itself. This ensures that even
when a device fails, the data layer can continue transmitting and
receiving information through protocol paths.
The combination of protocol and application layers presents
a unique set of challenges and opportunities for implementing
NBFTC. Within this domain, the concern shifts from pure hard-
ware failures to routing, congestion, and packet handling [51,52].
The protocol layer’s responsibility to provide reliable data de-
livery across the network introduces the need for dynamic and
adaptive algorithms that can tolerate transient failures. Tech-
niques such as adaptive routing and congestion control mecha-
nisms that can respond to non-malicious failures are pivotal in
these layers. Research in this area is often aimed at ensuring that
data can be successfully routed by application algorithms [53,54],
even in the presence of network failures or unexpected changes
in network topology.
The application between physical layers offers a different per-
spective on NBFTC. Here, the focus shifts towards ensuring con-
sistent data across distributed systems, taking into account both
software and hardware failures. Various consensus algorithms,
like PAXOS [45] and Raft [25], have been developed to handle
non-malicious failures within the abstract MAC layer. The ab-
stract MAC layer [27] is a useful tool to simplify the consensus
design. This tool means that the physical failure does not destroy
the application layer these algorithms work by managing replicas
of data across different nodes [55], ensuring that even if one
part of the system fails, the overall functionality remains intact
[56,57]. The challenges at this level often revolve around main-
taining consistency and performance, given the higher complex-
ity and increased chance of failure in a distributed environment.
Perhaps the most intriguing aspect of implementing NBFTC
across three layers is the potential for cross-layer interaction and
optimization [58]. By recognizing the dependencies and relation-
ships between different layers [59], it is possible to develop more
robust and efficient fault-tolerant systems. For example, informa-
tion about a failure at the physical layer could be communicated
to higher layers, allowing the system to adapt and compensate
before the failure affects overall performance. This cross-layer
communication opens doors to new methodologies and solutions,
allowing for a more nuanced and effective approach to fault
tolerance.
4. Byzantine fault tolerant consensus
Consensus establishment in wireless networks is of paramount
importance to ensure reliable and secure communication among
nodes, especially in the presence of malicious or faulty actors.
BFT mechanisms offer a promising solution to address these chal-
lenges. This section of the survey delves into the realm of Byzan-
tine fault-tolerant consensus in wireless networks, exploring its
nuances and applications. We begin by focusing on Byzantine
fault-tolerant consensus in single-layer wireless networks, where
we investigate fundamental concepts, protocols, and performance
evaluations.
4.1. Byzantine WFTC in single layer
There are several studies on various Byzantine fault mod-
els in wireless or edge computing domains for the Byzantine
fault-tolerant consensus problem in a single layer.
Let us commence with those studies on the Byzantine fault-
tolerant consensus problem at the physical layer. Byzantine at-
tacks at the physical layer predominantly manifest as unstable
network connections, such as signal tampering, signal blocking,
and timing attacks.
Considering Byzantine general problems in a distributed sys-
tem with any desired degree of fault-tolerance, Lamport [60]
described a general method. A simple clock-driven algorithm also
was executed instead of relying upon explicit timeouts with the
assumption of both reliable clock synchronization and a solution
to the Byzantine General Problem.

Assuming potential network failure in message delivery at
the physical layer, Castro et al. [61] introduced a replication
algorithm known as BFT in their work. This algorithm is de-
signed to facilitate the construction of highly available systems
capable of tolerating Byzantine faults. The recovery mechanism
within the algorithm enables it to withstand any number of
faults throughout the system’s lifespan, as long as fewer than
one-third of the replicas become faulty within a limited window
of vulnerability. To implement the first Byzantine-fault-tolerant
NFS file system, the authors employed the BFT implementation
as a generic program library. The BFT library and BFS perform
well because the library incorporates several important opti-
mizations, the most important of which is the use of symmetric
cryptography to authenticate messages. The performance eval-
uation results indicate that BFS exhibits a performance range
from 2% faster to 24% slower when compared to non-replicated
production implementations of the NFS protocol.
In accordance with the communication failure model, Santoro
et al. [29] conducted an investigation into the binary consen-
sus problem, which involves only two input values, 0 and 1.
Their study aimed to establish limitations on the number of
dynamic faults that render any meaningful form of agreement,
including strong majority, unattainable. Additionally, they pro-
vided constraints on the number of dynamic faults under which
meaningful agreement, including unanimity, remains achievable.
Remarkably, these constraints are demonstrated to be precise
for a wide-ranging category of network topologies, encompassing
hypercubes, toruses, rings, and complete graphs. Moreover, the
authors successfully bridged the prior gap between the potential
and unfeasibility of non-trivial consensus within complete graphs
amidst the backdrop of dynamic Byzantine faults.
Considering significant challenges to the task of achieving
tight coordination among nodes due to the inherent unrelia-
bility of wireless ad hoc networks, Moniz et al. [28] directed
their attention toward the challenge of achieving Byzantine fault-
tolerant binary consensus within single-hop wireless ad hoc net-
works. Their work introduced an asynchronous Byzantine con-
sensus protocol named Turquois, engineered to withstand a dual
challenge of Byzantine nodes and dynamic omission transmission
faults that have the potential to disrupt communication among
correctly functioning nodes. A distinguishing feature contributing
to Turquois’s efficiency is its capability to withstand dynamic
message omissions. This attribute plays a pivotal role in optimiz-
ing the utilization of the wireless broadcasting medium. Notably,
the protocol is engineered to circumvent the need for resource-
intensive public-key cryptography during its typical operational
phases.
Kim et al. [62] aimed to solve the peers’ number tradeoff
problem in the vehicle-to-everything (V2X) network which got
especially challenging due to the mobility of nodes. In their
research, they introduced a mechanism designed to facilitate the
selection of an ideal subset of peers. This mechanism relies on
reinforcement learning (RL) techniques to ensure the resilience
of a Fabric-enabled V2X network against the dynamic effects of
node mobility. They formulated the RL process as a contextual
multi-armed bandit (MAB) problem.
In addition to the physical layer, Byzantine attacks also occur
at the network protocol layer. Numerous efforts are directed
toward addressing Byzantine fault-tolerant consensus issues at
the protocol layer.
Buterin et al. [30] studied the problem of the Byzantine be-
haviors at the protocol layer in a trustless system. In response to
this challenge, they introduced the Casper protocol, a security-
deposit-based Proof of Stake protocol specifically designed for
Ethereum v2.0. Ethereum is a blockchain-based distributed com-
puting platform and operating system. Casper represents a hybrid
consensus mechanism that amalgamates research in the field of
proof of stake algorithms with Byzantine fault-tolerant consensus
theory.
In the context of a UAV network operating in complex envi-
ronments, essentially functioning as an asynchronous Byzantine
distributed system, this network comprises light- weight nodes
vulnerable not only to external physical interference and network
attacks but also to the issue of dynamically generated error nodes
within the network. Given the resource constraints inherent in
UAV networks, Kong et al. [63] conducted a study focusing on
the trustworthiness of the participating UAVs. This study encom-
passes the timely identification and isolation of erroneous nodes.
To address these challenges, the authors introduced a lightweight
and asynchronous provable Byzantine fault-tolerant consensus
algorithm, referred to as LAP-BFT. LAP-BFT achieves the assess-
ment of a globally trusted network state by achieving consensus
on the local status data of nodes within an asynchronous generic
subset. Notably, the algorithm facilitates a lightweight and de-
pendable dissemination of distributed data among designated
agents. It efficiently concludes a multi-valued Byzantine consen-
sus protocol via external proofing in the form of a smart contract,
all while maintaining a computational complexity of O(1). Ul-
timately, LAP-BFT constructs an asynchronous shared subset of
locally trusted status data.
Furthermore, a multitude of research endeavors are currently
investigating Byzantine attacks occurring at the data layer.
Chen et al. [31] tackled some of the prevailing issues in exist-
ing consensus mechanisms employed in blockchain technology.
These issues encompass excessive information exchanges among
network nodes and the simplistic election strategy employed
for selecting the master node. In response to these challenges,
the authors introduced a consensus mechanism, named the Ring
Signature-Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (ATRS-PBFT) con-
sensus mechanism, which is based on the ATRS algorithm. ATRS-
PBFT effectively mitigates these challenges by reducing the num-
ber of required signatures and verifications, consequently en-
hancing the overall efficiency of the consensus mechanism. Fur-
thermore, ATRS-PBFT addresses Byzantine Faults occurring at
the information layer, such as information interception and data
tampering. To tackle these issues, the mechanism adopts a credit
hierarchy in the master node election strategy. Before selecting
the master node, it assesses the credit ratings of nodes within
the network, favoring nodes with higher credit ratings for the
role of master nodes. This approach significantly diminishes the
likelihood of malicious nodes ascending to the position of master
nodes, thus enhancing the overall security of the system.
4.2. Byzantine WFTC across multiple layers
Several sophisticated attacks, including Sybil attacks and ecl-
ipsing attacks, deviate from a rigid three-layer classification.
These attacks frequently necessitate the amalgamation of mul-
tiple layers of assault to achieve their objectives. Numerous
investigations have contemporaneously ventured into the domain
of cross-layer Byzantine scenarios within wireless networks
For instance, Gilad et al. [64] delved into the challenges posed
by both Eclipse attacks and Sybil attacks within the context
of blockchain technology. They present a novel cryptocurrency
called Algorand, specifically engineered to expedite transaction
confirmation, achieving confirmation times on the order of one
minute, and designed to scale effectively to accommodate a large
user base. To thwart the potential threat of Sybil attacks, Algorand
employs a mechanism that assigns a weight to each user, thereby
mitigating the risk of malicious actors flooding the network with
fraudulent identities. The cornerstone of Algorand’s consensus
mechanism is rooted in a Byzantine agreement protocol based

on Verifiable Random Functions (VRF). This protocol empowers
Algorand to efficiently and promptly reach consensus among
network users regarding the next set of transactions and the
creation of a new block. Importantly, this consensus is achieved
with low latency and without the possibility of creating forks
in the blockchain, enhancing the security and robustness of the

In a prospective large-scale dense wireless network scenario,
commonly encountered in future IoT deployments, Jiang et al. [65]
focused on the challenge of achieving low-delay and high-
throughput BFT consensus in permissionless systems, all while
addressing the vulnerability to Sybil attacks. To address these
challenges, they introduced a Sybil-resistant wireless network
coordinate-based Byzantine consensus mechanism, termed SEN-
ATE. This innovative approach has the capability to achieve real-
time consensus, with consensus delays of less than one second,
even within a network comprising hundreds of nodes. SENATE
mitigates the threat of Sybil attacks by capitalizing on the fact
that even a compromised or faulty node cannot convincingly
manipulate its wireless channel interactions with other nodes.
Consequently, the mechanism leverages unique wireless finger-
prints to distinguish and identify nodes, enhancing the security
and trustworthiness of the consensus process.
In the context of mobile networks, where the presence of
faults and agent mobility is commonplace and often unavoid-
able, Zou et al. [66] conducted research focused on addressing
the fault-tolerant consensus problem. In these networks, faulty
agents may not only fail by crashing but also attempt to propagate
incorrect information. To enhance the robustness of consensus in
such challenging environments, the researchers adopted the Non-
Orthogonal Multiple Access (NOMA) technique from 5G technol-
ogy. NOMA facilitates efficient data transmissions between agents
in mobile networks. Additionally, they proposed a distributed
algorithm specially designed to achieve fault-tolerant consensus
in mobile networks that are susceptible to Byzantine faults. This
algorithm is engineered to operate effectively in scenarios where
faulty agents can behave maliciously, ensuring the reliability and
accuracy of the consensus process.
Leng et al. [32] delved into the complexities surrounding
practical Byzantine fault-tolerant consensus algorithms, which
often grapple with issues such as elevated communication over-
head, compromised security, limited scalability, and challenges
in maintaining traceability. To address these multifaceted chal-
lenges, the researchers introduced a novel Byzantine fault-tolerant
consensus algorithm, namely the Generalized Practical Byzantine
Fault-Tolerant consensus algorithm (GPBFT). This algorithm is un-
derpinned by a framework that leverages dual administrators and
short group signatures. It is deliberately designed for practical
implementation across various domains, including e-commerce,
e-banking, e-voting, and e-auction systems, thereby showcasing
its versatility and applicability.
For the blockchain Byzantine tolerant consensus, Wang et al.
[67] studied the Byzantine failures at both the protocol layer and
the information layer. They proposed an innovative consensus
protocol called the Adaptive Byzantine Fault-Tolerant (AdBFT)
consensus protocol. AdBFT incorporates the notion of an opti-
mistic response assumption and introduces an adaptive approach
to consensus formation. As a result, the protocol is capable of
achieving consensus with a latency of 2∆in a stable state. No-
tably, AdBFT offers the advantage of being able to tolerate up to
half of the Byzantine failures within the network. This robust-
ness ensures security even in situations where the synchroniza-
tion among network participants is less stringent, enhancing the
reliability and resilience of the system.
Considering the 51% attack, Sybil attack, double-spending at-
tack, and jamming attack, Zhang et al. [68] conducted research
to design a Byzantine fault-tolerant consensus protocol which
is specifically tailored for use in single-hop wireless networks
where there are constraints related to signal-to-noise ratios.
They proposed a low-latency and reliable Byzantine fault-tolerant
consensus protocol LRBP. LRBP simultaneously fulfills two critical
properties: persistence and liveness. Persistence ensures that
once a transaction is confirmed in the blockchain, it cannot be
undone or invalidated. Liveness guarantees that the network will
eventually reach consensus on transactions, even in the presence
of Byzantine failures and network disruptions.
Jing et al. [13] made significant strides in addressing a chal-
lenging problem: achieving majority consensus in the presence
of Byzantine fault-prone edge devices, all while dealing with
the uncertainty of an unreliable wireless channel. Their work is
pioneering, as it takes into account the potential malicious faults
emanating from Byzantine devices across multiple layers of the
system, including the physical layer, protocol layer, and informa-
tion layer. They defined a novel (a, b)-majority consensus prob-
lem and developed a distributed protocol to realize a b-majority
agreement within Θ(n) time steps with high probability.
5. The applications of WFTC
With the continuous development of WFTC algorithms, their
scope of application has significantly expanded. Many studies
have proposed improved wireless fault-tolerant consensus algo-
rithms based on the characteristics of specific scenarios such as
the IoT, blockchain, and vehicular networks. Existing efforts often
explore how to achieve fault-tolerant consensus functionality
for specific application scenarios with low overhead, considering
both the characteristics of the scenarios and algorithmic costs.
This section takes various application scenarios as entry points
to introduce the significant applications and specific research
endeavors in the realm of WFTC.
Internet of Things. With the continuous advancement of
terminal device capabilities, the IoT technology has experienced
rapid development. However, the IoT itself is also confronted with
critical challenges in terms of low latency and data security. The
vast majority of IoT applications have stringent real-time require-
ments, necessitating the timely and accurate transmission and
processing of information among devices. In order to address the
aforementioned issues, fault-tolerant consensus algorithms for
wireless networks have emerged as pivotal technologies in estab-
lishing a low-latency and highly efficient consensus mechanism
for the IoT.
PBFT, a commonly employed consensus algorithm based on
state machine replication, finds application in various scenarios.
However, in the context of the IoT, the larger scale of nodes
and heightened sensitivity to overhead costs have led to the
predominant utilization of enhanced PBFT as a primary consen-
sus algorithm for IoT. Notably,
[69] introduce a position-based
scalable consensus protocol, denoted as G-PBFT. In G-PBFT, given
that a majority of IoT systems rely on stationary devices for data
collection and processing, devices with fixed positions typically
exhibit greater computational capabilities compared to mobile
counterparts. Capitalizing on this distinction, G-PBFT leverages
geographic information from stationary devices to attain con-
sensus. Nodes with relatively stable positions are chosen as en-
dorsers in the PBFT consensus process, thereby mitigating the
overhead incurred in transaction validation and recording. [70]
puts forth a scalable multi-layer PBFT consensus mechanism to
reduce the communication volume of single-layer PBFT consen-
sus, with an analysis of the node count at each layer when com-
munication volume is minimized. Furthermore, [71] introduces
an enhanced PBFT consensus mechanism based on K-medoids
clustering (K-medoids) called FIBFT, where nodes participating

In addition to the PBFT algorithm, there has been research ex-
ploring the incorporation of the RAFT algorithm into the context
of the IoT. Particularly noteworthy is the increasing prominence
of edge computing as a fundamental pillar of IoT technology. As
a result, numerous studies have delved into the development
of fusion algorithms that integrate edge computing with the
RAFT consensus algorithm. This integration aims to enable the
deployment of blockchain technology on trusted base stations
and gateways in a more cost-effective and efficient manner. The
ultimate objective is to provide IoT applications with stream-
lined and tamper-resistant edge data services, thereby enhancing
both efficiency and security. For instance, the weighted RAFT
consensus algorithm, as proposed in Ref. [72], is tailored for
IoT applications. This algorithm considers the resource condi-
tions of nodes within the RAFT consensus process, potentially
reducing system forwarding latency by up to 24%, according
to simulation results. Besides, [73] propose a complete end-to-
end long-range wide area network (LoRaWAN) system for secure
IoT applications. This system employs the RAFT consensus al-
gorithm to implement blockchain-based secure distributed data
management in the context of Long-Range Wide Area Network
(LoRaWAN) technology.
Blockchain. Blockchain serves as an immutable shared ledger,
utilized for recording transactions, tracking assets, and estab-
lishing trust. Currently, wireless networks are continuously ac-
commodating a vast array of heterogeneous devices and infras-
tructure, necessitating a comprehensive enhancement of their
spectrum, computational, and storage resource utilization, along-
side bolstered security measures. Simultaneously, the ongoing
scale, densification, and heterogeneity of wireless networks have
given rise to a series of trust crises and security challenges. In
this context, blockchain emerges as an intrinsic mechanism for
distributed trust, fostering multiparty collaboration. It embod-
ies characteristics such as transparency, immutability, end-to-
end traceability, and smart execution. Consequently, the appli-
cation of consensus algorithms within wireless network scenar-
ios supported by blockchain technology is progressively gaining
prominence.
[8] designed an analytical modeling framework for Practi-
cal Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT)-a consensus method for
wireless PBFT networks, and the solution can guarantee the min-
imum number of replica nodes required while still achieving the
protocol’s safety and liveliness. Despite presenting a particular
consensus mechanism, there are also several studies analysis and
compare the system efficiency and costs on wireless blockchain
networks implementations. Note that, the fault tolerant consen-
sus mechanism are the vital factor to decide the cost of the
network construction and maintenance cost. For example, [74]
present a comprehensive analysis on wireless blockchain net-
works (WBN) under various commonly used consensus mecha-
nisms. They give an answer to the question that how much com-
munication resource is needed for a specific wireless blockchain
networks.
[75] gives an in-depth performance analysis of the
wireless PBFT network using IEEE802.11 under different situa-
tions and compare the success probability, average transaction
confirmation delay and throughput.
Vehicular Networks. In the ever-evolving landscape of vehic-
ular technology, the concept of V2X communication has emerged
as a critical enabler for enhancing road safety, traffic efficiency,
and overall driving experience [76]. V2X refers to the seamless
exchange of information between vehicles and various entities
in their environment, including other vehicles, infrastructure,
pedestrians, cyclists, and even the cloud. In the realm of ad-
vanced vehicular technology, the convergence of Fault-Tolerant
Consensus mechanisms and V2X communication has emerged
as a pivotal research area with far-reaching implications. Fault-
Tolerant Consensus enhances the establishment of trust among
vehicular network participants, enabling effective cooperation
and data sharing while safeguarding against malicious behaviors.
[77] introduce an endorser selection mechanism based on
reinforcement learning techniques and propose BFT consensus
protocols with reinforcement learning techniques to ensure that
the consensus mechanisms can adapt to varying network condi-
tions, vehicle behaviors, and potentially malicious activities. [78]
using distributed fault-tolerant consensus algorithms to achieve
coordination among nearby vehicles and present a practical solu-
tion that tolerates both Byzantine fault and lossy communication.
Different from existing works, [79] aims applied consensus-based
fault-tolerant platooning for connected and autonomous vehicles.
The results show that the proposed solution can detect the faulty
position and speed information from vehicles and reinstate the
normal behavior of the platooning.
UAV network. Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs), commonly
referred to as drones, have evolved from niche technologies to
essential components of various industries. UAV networks in-
volve the deployment of multiple drones for diverse applications,
ranging from surveillance and monitoring to aerial photogra-
phy, disaster management, and even delivery services. As UAV
networks become more complex and integrated into critical op-
erations, consensus can be regard as a fundamental problem.
Most of the works regard the consensus on the latest status of
nodes in a UAV network as the classical asynchronous Byzantine
consensus problem and many efforts have been made to maintain
consensus and consistency among distributed nodes, even in the
presence of faults, failures, or malicious behavior. [80] proposes a
lightweight asynchronous provable Byzantine fault-tolerant con-
sensus method to reduce the communication overhead by split-
ting the set of local trusted state transactions and then dispers-
ing the reliable broadcast control transmission (DRBC). Further-
more, it introduces vector commitments to achieve multivalue
Byzantine consensus (PMVBA) for identity and data in a provable
manner.
6. Conclusions
This paper provided a comprehensive survey of fault-tolerant
consensus mechanisms in wireless networks, covering both non-
Byzantine and Byzantine faults. This survey systematically cate-
gorized these mechanisms based on their underlying principles,
communication models, and fault models. It investigated how
these mechanisms handle various types of faults, including com-
munication errors, node failures, and malicious attacks. It further
emphasized crucial application scenarios like sensor networks,
IoT implementations, and vehicular networks, in which fault-
tolerant consensus significantly contributes to guaranteeing reli-
able and accurate data dissemination. We hope that our proposed
fault tolerant consensus survey in wireless networks can shed
some light for researchers to develop more practical consensus
protocols.
